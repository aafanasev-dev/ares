# Planet Geography

A worldbuilding reference for an ocean-covered Mars analogue.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Basis

This planet uses **real Mars** as its physical and topographic base. Radius, gravity, rotation rate, axial tilt, orbital eccentricity and all surface topography are taken directly from Mars and MOLA elevation data. Place names are the real Martian ones.

Three changes are imposed:

1. The planet orbits at **1.02 AU** instead of 1.52 AU, giving Earth-like insolation.
2. It has a **1 bar Earth-like atmosphere** (N₂/O₂) at sea level.
3. **Sea level is set at +2.0 km above the Mars mean datum**, flooding 70% of the surface.

All elevations below are given relative to this new sea level. To convert back to standard MOLA values, add 2.0 km.

## 2. Climate

### 2.1 Global circulation

Rotation rate is Earth-like, so the Coriolis parameter is unchanged — but the planet's radius is only 53% of Earth's. The thermal Rossby number is therefore 3.7× larger, and the Hadley cells extend far poleward of Earth's.

| Feature | Earth | This planet |
|---|---|---|
| Hadley cell edge | 30° | **55–65°** |
| Subtropical dry belt | 20–35° | **45–60°** |
| Jet streams | Two per hemisphere | **One merged jet** |
| Jet latitude / altitude | 30° and 50°, 10 km | 60°, 35 km |
| Jet speed | 30–60 m/s | 40–90 m/s |
| Baroclinic wavenumber | 5–8 | **3** |
| Rossby deformation radius | 825 km | 810 km |
| Storm systems per hemisphere | 6–8 | **3** |

The practical consequence: two-thirds of the planet lies under steady trade easterlies, the desert belt sits at high latitude, and weather arrives as a small number of very large systems rather than a train of cyclones.

**The defining humidity fact:** absolute humidity is ~2.6× Earth's at the same relative humidity and temperature, because water vapour decays 2.6× more slowly with height. But total annual rainfall is energy-limited and therefore Earth-like. The same water arrives in roughly one-third as many events, each two to three times heavier. Every climate on this planet is more humid and more flashy than its Earth counterpart.

### 2.3 Precipitation character

- **Storm depth 35 km.** Convective available potential energy is Earth-like (weaker buoyancy, deeper column), so updraft speeds are normal but through a column three times taller. Anvils sit at 30 km and are visible from 400 km away.
- **Giant hail.** Terminal velocity scales as √g, so stones stay aloft with 1.6× less updraft and have 2.6× the column to grow in. Melon-sized hail is routine in continental storms.
- **Slow, large raindrops.** Fall speed is 1.6× lower and drops resist breakup to larger diameters.
- **No cold-season snow at most latitudes.** Sea-level freezing occurs only poleward of ~73°.

### 2.4 Seasons

Three independent cycles operate. Obliquity is 25.2°, but eccentricity is 0.093 — insolation varies 45% around the orbit, versus Earth's 7%.

**Clock 1 — Obliquity.** The familiar summer/winter cycle, strongest at high latitude. Damped everywhere by 2.6× atmospheric heat capacity and 70% ocean cover. Continental winters comparable to Siberia's are impossible.

**Clock 2 — Eccentricity.** In phase planet-wide. Currently **southern summer occurs at perihelion**. Because perihelion passage is fast, the south gets a short, fierce summer and a long, cold winter; the north gets a long, mild summer and a short, mild winter.

**Clock 3 — Circulation migration.** The ITCZ and the Tharsis monsoon. In the tropics this dominates completely: the seasonal signal is wet/dry, not warm/cold.

| Zone | Seasonality | Dominant driver |
|---|---|---|
| Equatorial wet | Weak, double rainfall peak | ITCZ crossing twice |
| Tharsis plateau | **Extreme** wet/dry | Cross-equatorial monsoon |
| Trade belt N | Mild | Ocean buffer + aphelion summer |
| Trade belt S | Sharp, hot short summer | Perihelion + land |
| Southern savanna | **Strongest on the planet** | Monsoon and perihelion aligned |
| Dry belt 45–60°S | Sharp thermal, erratic rain | Perihelion; rain episodic, not seasonal |
| Storm belt | Moderate (stormy / calm) | Jet position |
| North polar ocean | Weak thermal, strong ice cycle | Sea-ice extent |
| South polar cap | **Violent** | Perihelion melt vs. aphelion accumulation |

**No autumn colours.** Deciduous leaf drop here is forced by drought, not frost. Savanna and monsoon forests go bare in the dry season and green within days of the first rains. Wet-tropical and storm-coast forests are evergreen. The landscape signal is brown-to-green, never green-to-gold.

### 2.5 The precession cycle

The axis does not move and the poles do not swap. What precesses is the position of perihelion relative to the solstices. Full cycle ≈51,000 years; the hemispheric reversal occurs every **≈25,500 years**.

| | South at perihelion (current) | North at perihelion |
|---|---|---|
| South polar cap | Melting, calving, retreating | Growing; mild summers, no melt |
| Southern savanna | Violent monsoon, ~20 storms/yr | Gentle, spread-out wet season |
| Northern ocean | Buffered, mild | Warmer summers, less sea ice |
| Sea level | Lower | Higher |
| ITCZ mean position | Pulled south | Pulled north |

The system is asymmetric because land is in the south and ocean in the north. A land ice cap can grow without limit; an ocean pole only changes its sea-ice extent. The cycle is therefore **one ice sheet breathing in and out**, with everything else responding.

**Underlying instability:** with no large moon, obliquity varies chaotically on million-year timescales, plausibly between 15° and 45°. The 25,500-year rhythm rides on a much slower, unpredictable modulation — epochs of near-seasonlessness alternating with epochs where the poles receive more annual insolation than the equator.

### 2.6 Ocean

- **Surface gyres:** one enormous subtropical gyre per hemisphere, with the wind-stress curl line at 55–60° instead of Earth's 40°.
- **Western boundary currents:** narrow and fast (β is 1.85× Earth's) — 50–70 km wide.
- **Deep circulation: sluggish.** No large moon means tidal amplitude is ~1/3 Earth's, and tidal mixing over rough topography supplies much of the energy that drives abyssal upwelling. Warm poles mean little brine rejection.
- **Deep water formation** is Cretaceous-style: warm saline water sinking from subtropical evaporation zones. Deep ocean temperature 10–15 °C rather than 2 °C.
- **Anoxia is widespread** in enclosed and deep basins: Valles Marineris below a few hundred metres, Hellas below ~500 m, and much of the abyssal plain.
- **Salinity maximum** lies in the 45–60°N subsidence belt and in the Amazonis lee zone west of Tharsis.

### 2.7 Waves, tides and surge

| Parameter | vs. Earth |
|---|---|
| Significant wave height | 1.6–2.6× |
| Wavelength and period | 2.6× |
| Wave steepness (H/λ) | 1.0× |
| Swell group velocity | 1.0× |
| Tidal range | ~0.33× |
| Storm surge height | **2.6×** |

Typical open-ocean seas: 4–5.5 m at 10 m/s with a 19 s period; 22–35 m in a 25 m/s storm with a 48 s period. A 25 m surge floods inland by surge height divided by coastal slope — **25 km at 1 m/km**, not hundreds. Only the Arabia shallows margin and Hesperia Planum have slopes flat enough for surge to reach hundreds of kilometres inland.

The waves-to-tides ratio is decisive for coastal form: **no estuaries, no tidal flats, no funnel deltas.** All river mouths are wave-dominated and strongly cuspate.

## 3. Landmarks

### 3.1 Tharsis — Continent B

**Extent:** ~40°N to 45°S, spanning the entire trade belt. Area roughly 20 M km².

Because it blocks the full width of the tropical easterlies, air genuinely cannot cross it at low latitude. The trades split and are deflected poleward around both tips, producing permanent boundary jets near 45°N and 45°S offshore.

**Climate structure, east to west:**

| Segment | Elevation | Climate |
|---|---|---|
| Lunae Planum coastal step | +0.5 km | Lowland rainforest, 2500 mm |
| Eastern escarpment | +0.5 to +5 km | Cloud forest, 3000–4000 mm, stacked vertical bands |
| Plateau interior (Syria, Solis) | +3 to +6 km | Cold dry steppe, <100 mm, 0.78–0.83 bar |
| Volcanic cones | +12 to +16 km | Alpine desert, then ice above 11 km |
| Inter-cone gaps | +4 to +5 km | Permanent gap jets, dune corridors |
| Western descent (Daedalia) | +2 to 0 km | Rain shadow, arid |
| Amazonis coast | 0 km | Dry coast; shadow falls mainly on open ocean |

**The plateau interior is the planet's most habitable highland.** At 0.78–0.83 bar and ~11 °C mean at the equator, it is breathable, temperate and dry — a Tibet analogue that is genuinely pleasant rather than hostile. Water comes from glacier melt off Ascraeus and Arsia, arriving as seasonal pulses that thread the plateau with gallery forest and die into playas.

**Monsoon:** the plateau straddles the equator, so its heating flips hemispheres annually and drives a **cross-equatorial monsoon**. With southern summer at perihelion, the southern half receives the violent version.

### 3.2 The three cones

Snowline sits at ~11 km at these latitudes, which divides them:

| Cone | Elevation | Ice cap |
|---|---|---|
| Ascraeus Mons | +16.2 km | Permanent, ~5 km thick |
| Arsia Mons | +15.7 km | Permanent, ~4.5 km thick |
| Pavonis Mons | +12.0 km | **Marginal** — appears and vanishes with obliquity cycles |

They are standalone shields, not a range, so airflow splits *around* them as well as over them. Each produces a windward rain crescent and a tapering lee wake. The two gaps between them — roughly 700 km apart — carry permanently accelerated gap jets that have scoured corridors of star dunes up to 500 m high with 15 km spacing.

Ice at low gravity deforms more slowly for a given thickness, so these caps are thick, sluggish and long-lived. Summit pressure at 16 km is 0.48 bar — equivalent to Earth's 6.5 km. Reachable, but only from the air.

### 3.3 Valles Marineris

**The most important single feature on the planet.**

A 4,000 km sound, 100–200 km wide, floor at −7 km, walls rising 3–5 km above the water. It opens eastward through Eos and Capri Chasmata into Chryse and the northern ocean, and dead-ends westward into the Tharsis massif.

**Why it matters climatically:** moist easterly trade air is funnelled up a narrowing 4,000 km channel with no exit but upward. The inner walls receive continuous orographic rainfall, and the western head carries a permanent rain wall. This is the wettest place on the planet — wetter than either island volcano.

**Water column:** 7 km deep, narrow, poorly ventilated, with a sluggish global overturning. Anoxic below a few hundred metres, with a thin productive surface layer. Pressure at the floor is 260 bar — the equivalent of Earth's 2.6 km — so air-breathing divers and swim-bladdered fish can work depths that would be lethal on Earth.

**The side chasmata are the key habitat.** The main axis is windy, because the trades funnel along it. But Melas, Candor, Ophir and Hebes are sheltered, dead-calm, permanently misted and warm at 14°S. They are the only places on the planet that combine maximum shelter, maximum moisture and tropical warmth.

**Coastal note:** the sound is the only large body of water on the planet sheltered from open-ocean waves and surge. Its head holds the only quiet, stable, fine-grained delta anywhere.

### 3.4 Olympus Mons

**+19.2 km, 18.65°N — the highest point on the planet, and an island.**

Base roughly 600 km across; the aureole region around it lies below sea level, separating it from Tharsis. Summit pressure 0.35 bar, equivalent to Earth's 8.8 km — an Everest-class summit in breathability terms.

Counterintuitively, it is **not especially wet**. Air reaching it has already crossed Tharsis and descended into the Amazonis lee, and only partly remoistens over the ocean. Lower flanks are moderately forested rather than rainforest.

Above 11 km it carries a permanent tropical ice cap roughly 8 km thick vertically, with glaciers descending into warm forest. Downwind it throws a cloud-free wake ~2,000 km long with a von Kármán vortex street — 10% of the planet's circumference, enough that its wind-stress curl spins up its own ocean countercurrent.

### 3.5 Elysium Mons

**+12.1 km, 25.0°N** — a second island volcano, roughly 2,000 km across, 6,000 km east of Olympus in the northern trade belt.

Unlike Olympus, it sits in clean oceanic trade flow with no upwind obstruction. Its eastern flank is very wet; forest runs from sea level to 6 km. Marginal ice cap near the summit.

Olympus and Elysium are the **only two land masses in the entire northern hemisphere ocean**, which makes them the sole nesting sites for anything pelagic. This is a severe ecological bottleneck (see §4.7).

### 3.6 Alba Mons

**+4.8 km, 40.5°N.** A vast, extremely low-angle shield on the northern edge of Tharsis, facing the northern ocean.

Gentle slopes plus a maritime exposure give it long, low-gradient, radial dendritic river networks — **the best river country on the planet**, and the largest single forest by area. But it is exposed: no shelter means a uniform 60–100 m canopy rather than giants.

### 3.7 Hellas Planitia

**42.4°S, 70.5°E. Floor at −10.2 km — the deepest point on the planet.**

At the +2.0 km datum, Hellas is a broad gulf connected to the ocean through the degraded northeastern rim near Dao Vallis. The 2,300 km basin sits squarely in the 45–60° subsidence belt.

- Surface waters flush enough to remain near normal salinity
- Exchange across a shallow sill cannot ventilate 10 km of depth — **euxinic below roughly 500 m**: anoxic, sulfidic, permanently stratified
- Shores are arid: subsiding air warming at 3.7 K/km gives a +10 to +15 K anomaly over the surrounding highlands, with coastal fog from the gulf
- Shoreline sabkha belts from decadal surge flooding

A Black Sea at ten times the scale, reached through one strait.

### 3.8 Argyre Planitia

**49.7°S, 316°E. Floor at −7.2 km.** An 800 km gulf connected northward through the Uzboi–Ladon–Margaritifer corridor, forming a 2,000 km fjord-like inlet reaching deep into the southern highlands. Same stratification behaviour as Hellas at smaller scale.

### 3.9 Continent A — the southern crescent

Built from Terra Cimmeria, Terra Sirenum, Aonia Terra and the higher parts of Noachis, wrapping around the south pole and cut by the Hellas and Argyre gulfs. Arabia Terra and most of Noachis are drowned at this datum.

**The defining feature is heavy cratering.** Water does not build dendritic drainage here — it fills a crater, overflows the lowest rim point, and fills the next. This produces **beaded drainage**: chains of lakes linked by short, steep spillway gorges, each gorge an actively retreating knickpoint.

| Latitude | Character |
|---|---|
| 20–45°S | Wet eastern shores, drier interiors, freshwater crater lakes, savanna |
| 45–60°S | Subsidence desert. Endorheic craters become salt pans and soda lakes |
| 60–72°S | Storm coast: westerlies, three giant cyclones, temperate rainforest |
| 72–90°S | Planum Australe ice cap |

The beaded chains are also the planet's **salt disposal system**. Where a chain has integrated through to the ocean, sea-salt aerosol and surge salt are exported. Where it terminates in a closed basin, that basin becomes the sink — which is exactly why the 45–60°S interior fills with salt while the 20–40°S coasts stay fresh.

### 3.10 Planum Australe

**+0.5 to +1.5 km, centred 83°S.** The only permanent land ice on the planet.

But it sits inside the southern storm belt, making it **maritime rather than polar-desert**: mean around −6 °C, wet-based, heavy snowfall, fast outlet glaciers calving icebergs. Patagonian in character, not Antarctic.

Outlet margins carry braided sandur plains, jökulhlaups, and exceptionally wide, shallow outwash. It responds sharply to precession — melting and retreating now, growing in 25,500 years.

### 3.11 The northern ocean

Effectively unbroken from 45°N to the pole, interrupted only by Olympus and Elysium.

- **12,000 km of uninterrupted easterly fetch** in the trade belt produces the largest seas on the planet
- The 45–60°N subsidence zone is clear, calm, high-evaporation — the salinity maximum
- The 60–72°N storm track is the cleanest on the planet, with three giant cyclones and no land to break them
- A **circumpolar current** can form, thermally isolating the pole. Perennial sea ice in a core region, seasonal ice to ~70°N, no land ice

### 3.12 Arabia shallows and Hesperia Planum

The two flattest surfaces on the planet, both within a metre or two of sea level.

These are the only places where 25 m storm surge reaches hundreds of kilometres inland. Combined with 2.6× capillary rise (3–8 m fringe) and 2.6× slower soil drainage, salt cannot be leached out. The result is permanent **sabkha**: damp saline crusts at 25 °C and 60% humidity supporting nothing. The planet's true wastelands are not dry — they are salt.

## 4. Flora and Fauna

### 4.1 Governing scaling laws

| Property | Scaling | Factor |
|---|---|---|
| Air density | independent of g | **1.0×** |
| Tree height limit (hydraulic and buckling) | 1/g | 2.6× |
| Flyer size limit | 1/g | 2.6× |
| Stall speed | √g | 0.62× |
| Terminal velocity | √g | 0.62× |
| Jump height | 1/g | 2.6× |
| Maximum ground traction (μg) | g | **0.38×** |
| Hydrostatic blood pressure cost | g | 0.38× |
| Turgor support height | 1/g | 2.6× |
| Small-particle settling velocity | g | 0.38× |
| Ocean pressure per km depth | g | 0.38× |
| Capillary rise in soil | 1/g | 2.6× |

**The most underrated:** traction. Maximum acceleration equals μg, so nothing on this planet can sprint or turn sharply. Sprint-and-jink predation does not exist. Ambush and endurance pursuit are the only viable strategies, and every animal moves in slow, floating arcs with long hang time.

### 4.2 Trees

Both limits on Earth tree height — hydraulic (ρgh sap lift) and mechanical (self-weight buckling) — scale with gravity, and both permit exactly 2.6×. **Earth's 115 m redwood becomes a 300 m tree with a 12 m trunk.**

But wind force is Earth-normal while weight and root anchorage are 2.6× weaker. So the forest landscape is **bimodal** in a way Earth's is not: giants exist only where shelter is genuine, and the transition is abrupt. Cross a ridge and go from 250 m cathedral forest to 3 m wind-pruned scrub within a few hundred metres.

Consequences:
- Enormous buttresses and root-grafted plates; groves behave as single mechanical units
- **Catastrophic domino blowdowns** after major storms, leaving even-aged recovery mosaics tens of km across
- Stratification stretched over 300 vertical metres; the floor is very dark
- Heavy epiphyte loading
- **Wind dispersal dominates** — seeds fall 1.6× slower and ride the trades for hundreds of km. Islands colonise fast
- Fires spread sideways rather than by convective column; smoke hangs low and travels enormously far

### 4.3 Forest habitats

| Location | Canopy | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Valles Marineris side chasmata** | **300 m** | Tallest on the planet. Maximum shelter, fog-fed, tropical |
| East Tharsis escarpment | 60–250 m | Seven vertical km of stacked bands; giants in gorges, scrub on interfluves |
| Alba Mons | 60–100 m | Largest by area; uniform, exposed, no giants |
| Cimmeria / Sirenum coasts | 30–150 m | Crater ring forests (below) |
| Elysium island | 100–200 m | Very wet eastern flank; species-poor, giant endemics |
| Olympus island | 40–90 m | Moderate; air arrives dried from the Tharsis lee |
| Southern storm coast | 20–40 m | Temperate rainforest, dense and low; windy except in fjord heads |
| South polar margin | 1–4 m | Krummholz to ~73°S |
| Tharsis plateau | 20–60 m | Gallery forest only, along glacier-fed rivers |

**Crater ring forests** are the signature landscape of Continent A. Each impact bowl is a pocket of still air, so a cratered plain reads as a field of green discs — 150 m canopy in every bowl, knee-high scrub on every rim, sharply bounded. Gallery forest threads the spillway gorges between them.

### 4.4 Savanna and desert

**Savanna** (15–40°S interiors, Tharsis plateau margins, Elysium lee):
- Grasses reach 5–8 m; a stem that topples at 3 m on Earth stands to 8
- Scattered trees 40–80 m, flat-crowned, heavily buttressed, widely spaced
- Dry-season RH 35–50%, but with 2.6× absolute humidity it feels muggy year-round
- Diurnal range only 12–15 K, so no heavy dewfall and little morning fog despite the humidity
- 700–1100 mm delivered in 20–30 days
- Dust devils 5–10 km tall — weak buoyancy, but an enormous column to work with

**Three desert types**, none of them a Sahara:

| Type | Location | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Subsidence | 45–60°S interior | **Cool** desert, 8–14 °C, 200–400 mm in 3–4 cloudbursts. Brine pools, succulents adapted to floods not drizzle |
| Rain shadow | Tharsis plateau interior | Driest on the planet, <100 mm, RH <15%, 0.78–0.83 bar, ~11 °C. Cold, thin, bright |
| Gap jet | Between the three cones | Permanently scoured. 500 m star dunes, 15 km spacing |

**There are no coastal fog deserts.** Namib and Atacama analogues require cold upwelling under subtropical subsidence — but here the subsidence belt is at 45–60° and the trades rain on coasts everywhere. All deserts are interior or rain-shadow.

### 4.5 Rivers as habitat

Flow velocity is 62% of Earth's for the same slope, so channels are 1.6× wider for the same discharge — **width-to-depth ratios 1.6× Earth's**, pushing nearly everything past the braiding threshold. Rivers run persistently turbid because silt settles 1.6–2.6× more slowly.

Waterfalls over ~500 m atomize into drifting mist before landing, so they undercut their plunge pools very little and survive as landmarks for geological ages. Canyon walls stand 2.6× taller before failing, giving 2–3 km slot gorges.

River mouths are wave-dominated and cuspate, with no estuaries or tidal flats anywhere except the sheltered head of Valles Marineris.

### 4.6 Marine life

Buoyancy is gravity-independent, so swimming body plans look broadly Earth-like. What changes is the ocean's vertical structure:

- **Pressure rises 2.6× more slowly.** Gas-filled swim bladders remain viable to depths that would crush an Earth fish. **2–3 km daily vertical migrations are routine** with no barotrauma; air-breathing divers reach 3–4 km
- **Marine snow falls 2.6× slower**, so organic particles are reworked in the water column instead of exported. Midwater suspension feeders are abundant; the seafloor is starved
- **The deep sea is largely dead** — sluggish overturning, warm deep water, and widespread anoxia. Biomass concentrates in the top few hundred metres
- **The surf zone is the harshest habitat on the planet.** 25 m waves with feeble tides mean there is barely an intertidal zone, just a wide permanently pounded splash zone. Shore organisms are low, thick and ferociously attached

### 4.7 Flyers

Wing bending stress scales as Lg, so linear size scales as 1/g: **a 20 m wingspan at roughly one tonne.** Stall speed drops 1.6×, so these giants loiter slowly, land almost anywhere, and take off with a hop.

Thermals are broad, gentle and extremely tall, and at 10 km the air is still 0.62 bar (Earth's 10 km is 0.26 bar). Soaring at 10–15 km is ordinary.

**Primary habitat: Valles Marineris.** 4,000 km of continuous 4 km cliff generating permanent ridge lift, with constant axial trade flow. Cliff-nesting soarers that almost never flap, ranging the full length of the sound and out over the ocean.

**Bottleneck:** in the northern hemisphere, Olympus and Elysium are the only two nesting sites in an ocean the size of a hemisphere. Whatever breeds there is spectacular and spectacularly vulnerable.

In the 300 m forests, terminal velocity is 1.6× lower, so falling is survivable and gliding is cheap. **Gliding vertebrates are the dominant arboreal body plan**, not a curiosity.

### 4.8 Land animals

| Trait | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Bone stress 2.6× lower | Limbs are stilts, not pillars; giants look spindly |
| Structural size limit | Several hundred tonnes; food supply binds first |
| Blood pressure cost | A 20–30 m neck costs what a giraffe's 6 m costs here |
| Jump height | 6 m standing leaps from deer-sized animals |
| Stride frequency ∝ √(g/L) | Slow, floating gait with long hang time |
| Grip and adhesion | Do not scale with g — 2.6× better in relative terms |

The overall impression is **languid**. A hundred-tonne browser walks at a stately pace on legs that look far too thin. Nothing looks urgent. Because claws and pads work disproportionately well, large animals climb easily — which is how anything reaches a 300 m canopy. Climbers and gliders, not neck-length browsers.

**Acoustics:** the speed of sound is unchanged, but the atmospheric waveguide is 2.6× thicker and the ocean sound channel 2.6× broader. On a planet only 21,300 km around, infrasonic calls could plausibly carry hemisphere-wide. Long-distance acoustic contact, not sight or scent, is the dominant social sense.

### 4.9 Forms impossible on Earth

At 10 km altitude this planet still offers 0.62 bar, 0.79 kg/m³, ~0 °C and roughly six times Earth's absolute humidity at that height, because the cold trap sits near 26 km rather than 11. Earth's sky at 10 km is a freezing desert. This one is a damp, dense, temperate ocean of air 35 km thick.

**The permanent aerial biosphere.** Small-particle residence time rises from days to months (2.6× slower settling through a 3× deeper convective column) — long enough to close a life cycle without landing.

| Tier | Altitude | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Gossamer photosynthesizers | 5–12 km | Filamentous or sheet-like, metres across, grams in mass. Visible as a green haze, thickening over convective regions, thinning over the 45–60° subsidence belt |
| Aerial grazers | 5–12 km | Tens of grams, kite-membraned, riding thermals indefinitely, feeding on the mats |
| Giant soarers | 3–15 km | 20 m span, feeding on both tiers |

Earth has aeroplankton. It has no aerial *food chain*.

**Sky-nets.** Prey flies 1.6× slower and weighs 2.6× less, so kinetic energy at cruise drops 2.6× — a web of given strength can arrest a flyer 2.6× more massive than on Earth. Earth's limit is not silk strength but that no web can stop a bird without destroying itself. Here it can.

In the dead-calm side chasmata of Valles Marineris, sessile ambush organisms string catenary nets hundreds of metres across between 4 km walls, harvesting soarers and drifting mats. Structures visible from orbit, built by animals, functioning as aerial reefs with their own encrusting communities. **Habitat: Melas, Candor, Ophir and Hebes Chasmata exclusively** — nowhere else combines the shelter, the anchor points and the slow prey.

**Stilt-waders.** A 30 m blood column costs what a giraffe's 11 m costs; buckling length gains another 1.6×. Combined with a 300 m forest this gives a browser **60–80 m tall on four absurdly thin legs**, feeding at mid-canopy. It cannot run — traction is μg — so it walks or it dies. Habitat: the Valles Marineris chasmata and the sheltered gorges of the east Tharsis escarpment.

**Turgor giants.** Required internal pressure for a hydrostatic skeleton scales as ρgh, so the same turgor supports 2.6× the height. This permits **boneless land animals the size of a bus** — slow, fluid-supported, no rigid skeleton, flowing rather than walking, pooling into a low dome under wind. Habitat: the southern savanna, where nothing can sprint anyway and being slow costs little.

**Abyssal air-breathers.** The Valles Marineris floor at 7 km sits at 260 bar — the pressure an Earth diver meets at 2.6 km. Earth's sperm whale reaches 2 km and is near its limit; here that is a routine commute, and the thick midwater layer provides the food to justify it.

### 4.10 Evolutionary dynamics

**The crater ring forests are a species pump on a 25,500-year cycle.** Each bowl is a sheltered island of tall forest connected only by gallery corridors along the beaded drainage chains. When the southern monsoon is violent, the chains flow, the corridors are forested, and basins exchange. When it is gentle, the chains break into salt pans and each bowl is isolated.

Populations split, diverge in isolation, then meet again 25,000 years later. Continent A should be the most speciose region on the planet, with clusters of closely-related forms in adjacent craters — dwarf and giant variants of the same lineage separated by a single rim.

Other precession-driven consequences:

- **Coastlines never stabilise.** Sea level tracks the south polar cap. Coastal lineages are generalists, mobile and salinity-tolerant; specialised shallow-marine endemics cannot persist
- **The aerial biosphere migrates** as the ITCZ shifts, while the only two nesting islands stay put — a severe recurring bottleneck for pelagic soarers
- **Polar margin life is repeatedly extirpated** and recolonised from lower latitudes. Nothing there is ancient

**The deep pattern:** 25,500 years is roughly 1,000 generations for a long-lived vertebrate — long enough to diverge substantially, not long enough to complete reproductive isolation in most lineages. The planet is full of **incipient species that keep re-merging**: hybrid swarms, ring-species complexes, clinal messes rather than clean boundaries.

And because the cycle is regular rather than chaotic, organisms can evolve to *anticipate* it. Earth's glacial cycles are irregular enough that adaptation is reactive. Here, a lineage persisting through a few dozen cycles is under selection for strategies tuned to a known period: seed banks dormant for millennia, obligate alternating life stages, migration routes encoded rather than learned.
