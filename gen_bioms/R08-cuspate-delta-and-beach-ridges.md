## Prompt

Create a photorealistic 16:9 landscape photograph in the style of a nature documentary: high drone shot looking along
the coast, wide-angle lens, natural light, sharp detail, believable scale.

Setting: the planet Ares, real Mars terrain turned into an Earth-like world with oceans, NOT the red Mars of space
photos. The sky is blue with white clouds, the air is breathable, the sun looks the same size as from Earth and there
is no moon. Gravity is only 0.38 g, so life grows about 2.6× taller and lighter than on Earth: trees reach hundreds of
metres on huge buttresses, big animals walk slowly on long, very thin legs, and flyers have giant wingspans. The
planet is small, so the horizon is close. Rain falls in few but very heavy storms. All plants and animals are alien
but believable: no Earth species, no dinosaurs.

Scene: afternoon over the mouth of a great river on an open, wave-beaten coast. The delta is a single blunt, pointed
cusp of sand and gravel pushed out into the sea, built by the waves rather than by the river: no branching fan of
channels, no marshy estuary, no mudflats, no tide line. Huge long-period swell four to five metres high marches in at
an angle and breaks in long white lines all along the front, driving sediment down the coast into a series of long,
parallel beach ridges that curve away into the distance like the growth rings of the shore. Between the ridges lie
narrow strips of older land: brackish ponds, abandoned channels and dark swamp forest, the vegetation on each ridge
visibly older and taller than the one in front of it. The turbid river spills brown water into green sea at the point.
Long-winged flyers patrol the surf line. Bright, windy, salt-hazy air with scattered cloud. Composition: the cusp of
the delta in the middle distance and the ridges sweeping from it to the far corner of the frame.

No people, buildings, roads, vehicles, spacecraft, text, labels, watermarks or borders. No red or orange sky. No Earth
animals.

- Show the flood season at low level: the brown river pushing hard against the incoming swell at the point.
